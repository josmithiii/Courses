# Early Buddhist Philosophy — From the Four Noble Truths to Dependent Origination

**Learner profile:** Educated adult, possibly raised in a Western
religious tradition, comfortable with abstract ideas and willing to
hold unfamiliar terms in mind. No prior exposure to Buddhism
required, and no Pali or Sanskrit needed — terms are introduced
cleanly, with diacritics, and the English gloss is given every time
until it sticks. The learner is interested in **philosophy**, not in
adopting a practice — so meditation instructions, monastic rules,
ritual, and devotional material are deliberately out of scope. We
are reading the early canon the way one reads Plato: as a tightly
argued worldview with a diagnostic structure, not as scripture.
~1 hour per day. One concept at a time. Every concept is verified
with a small exercise — usually applying a doctrine to an
introspectable moment of experience — before moving on.

**Pace philosophy:** It is completely fine to spend multiple days on
one topic. The topic numbers below are *topics*, not days.
`progress.md` tracks the real position.

**Sources.** The course works from the **Pali Canon** (Tipiṭaka),
specifically the *Sutta Piṭaka* — the five Nikāyas (DN, MN, SN, AN,
KN). We use the free translations on **SuttaCentral**
(suttacentral.net, Bhikkhu Sujato) as primary, with
**Access to Insight** (accesstoinsight.org, Ṭhānissaro et al.) as
secondary. A small number of named suttas are read in full; most
doctrine is taught with short pericope quotations. The Abhidhamma
gets one phase as a sketch, not a deep dive.

**Scope note: what this course is *not*.** It is not a course on
Buddhist *practice* (meditation, ethics-as-training, the monastic
path) — that's a separate course. It is not a survey of Mahāyāna or
Vajrayāna — those build on these foundations and get their own
courses. It does not advocate for Buddhism as a religion; the goal
is to make the **doctrinal architecture click** so that the
Mahāyāna course, and any later reading of the canon, lands on a
prepared mind.

**End state.** By the last lesson, the learner can: state the Four
Noble Truths in their own words and explain why they form a
medical-style diagnosis; distinguish the three meanings of *dukkha*
and the three marks of existence; deconstruct a moment of
experience into the five aggregates; trace the twelve-link chain of
dependent origination forward (arising) and backward (cessation);
explain *anattā* (non-self) without falling into the standard
Western misreadings; say carefully what *nibbāna* is and isn't, and
why the Buddha said little positive about it; and read a Pali sutta
in English translation knowing what each technical term is doing.

## Phase 0 — Orientation
- **0.1** What "early Buddhism" means. The 5th-c.-BCE Indian context:
  the *śramaṇa* movement, the Vedic religion it pushed against, what
  the Buddha was reacting *to*. Why we read the Pali Canon (Theravāda
  preserved it; the texts are the closest thing we have to the early
  teachings, though redaction is real).
- **0.2** How to read a sutta. Standard opening formula
  ("Evaṃ me sutaṃ — Thus have I heard…"); the repetition that looks
  tedious but reflects oral transmission; the *pericope* (stock
  passage) as a unit of meaning. The five Nikāyas (DN, MN, SN, AN,
  KN) and what each is for.
- **0.3** Terminology convention. Pali first, with diacritics; English
  gloss every time until the term is owned. Sanskrit form noted on
  first appearance (e.g. *dukkha* / Skt. *duḥkha*). Why we use the
  Pali rather than the Sanskrit forms here.
- **0.4** Tooling: open SuttaCentral, find SN 56.11, see the
  structure. No software install needed beyond a browser.

## Phase 1 — The Four Noble Truths (the diagnostic frame)
- **1.1** The medical metaphor: *the Buddha as a physician*. Diagnosis
  (there is a disease), etiology (its cause), prognosis (it can be
  cured), prescription (the treatment). The Four Noble Truths *are*
  this four-step structure — recognizing it changes how the whole
  canon reads.
- **1.2** **1st truth — *dukkha*.** Why "suffering" is a bad
  translation. The three senses: *dukkha-dukkha* (ordinary pain),
  *vipariṇāma-dukkha* (the dissatisfaction of change — even the good
  passes), *saṅkhāra-dukkha* (the structural unease of conditioned
  existence). Pin to a concrete example the learner picks.
- **1.3** **2nd truth — *samudaya* (origin).** *Taṇhā* (craving /
  thirst) as the engine. Three flavors: craving for sensual pleasure
  (*kāma-taṇhā*), for existence/becoming (*bhava-taṇhā*), and for
  non-existence (*vibhava-taṇhā*). The third is the subtle one —
  worth time.
- **1.4** **3rd truth — *nirodha* (cessation).** What ceases is the
  *craving*, not experience itself. This is where Western readers
  often go wrong ("Buddhism says life is suffering and the goal is to
  stop existing") — flag the misreading and demolish it.
- **1.5** **4th truth — *magga* (the path).** The Noble Eightfold
  Path as a *map*, not as instructions (we're doing philosophy):
  right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort,
  mindfulness, concentration. Note the three divisions: *paññā*
  (wisdom), *sīla* (ethics), *samādhi* (mental cultivation).
- **1.6** **Sutta reading: SN 56.11 — *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*
  ("Setting the Wheel of Dhamma in Motion"),** the first sermon. The
  whole frame in one short text. Read it together; point out where
  each of the four truths appears, and where the "should be fully
  understood / abandoned / realized / developed" formula appears
  (the *twelve aspects*: each truth has a task).

## Phase 2 — The Three Marks of Existence (*tilakkhaṇa*)
- **2.1** *Anicca* (impermanence). Not "all things end eventually"
  (trivial) but "every conditioned phenomenon is in continuous
  arising and passing." The river, the flame, the candle — classical
  similes, used carefully.
- **2.2** *Dukkha* as the second mark — distinct from the 1st Noble
  Truth's usage. Here: *because* everything is impermanent, no
  conditioned phenomenon can be a stable basis for satisfaction. The
  link between marks 1 and 2.
- **2.3** *Anattā* (non-self) — the radical claim. Not "there is no
  person" (straw man) and not the Hindu *ātman* with the prefix
  flipped. The argument: nowhere in the five aggregates do we find a
  permanent, unchanging, autonomous *owner*. Two careful
  re-statements: (i) the *no-ātman* reading (denial of a Vedic
  metaphysical self), (ii) the *anti-essentialist* reading (the
  self-concept is a useful fiction superimposed on processes). The
  course will keep both alive.
- **2.4** **Sutta reading: SN 22.59 — *Anattalakkhana Sutta*
  ("The Discourse on the Not-Self Characteristic"),** the second
  sermon. The Buddha's actual argument: if X were self, X would be
  controllable and would not lead to *dukkha*; but X is uncontrollable
  and does lead to *dukkha*; therefore X is not-self. Run this
  argument over each of the five aggregates.

## Phase 3 — The Five Aggregates (*pañca-khandhā*)
- **3.1** Why aggregates: a *method* for deconstructing the self. Any
  moment of experience can be split into five strands; doing so
  starves the "I-maker" of a place to stand.
- **3.2** *Rūpa* — form / materiality. The body, the four great
  elements (earth/solidity, water/cohesion, fire/temperature,
  air/motion) — not chemistry, but *experienced* materiality. Plus
  the derived material qualities.
- **3.3** *Vedanā* — feeling-tone. Not "emotion." Every contact
  carries one of three tones: pleasant, unpleasant, neutral. This is
  the precise spot where craving gets a foothold (link to Phase 4).
- **3.4** *Saññā* — perception / recognition. The labeling that says
  "this is a bell," "this is red." Where concept enters experience.
- **3.5** *Saṅkhārā* — volitional formations. The hardest aggregate
  to translate cleanly: dispositions, intentions, mental
  constructions, the *karmically active* element of mind.
  Conventionally rendered "formations." We'll come back to it under
  *kamma* in Phase 5.
- **3.6** *Viññāṇa* — consciousness, in the specific sense of "bare
  awareness of an object via a sense-base." Six kinds, one per
  sense (including the mind as a sense). Not a Cartesian observer —
  this distinction matters.
- **3.7** **Exercise spine: aggregate analysis.** Take a 10-second
  ordinary experience (hearing a sound, tasting tea) and decompose it
  into the five aggregates explicitly. Repeat across the phase until
  the move is automatic.

## Phase 4 — Dependent Origination (*paṭiccasamuppāda*)
- **4.1** The general principle, in the canonical formula:
  *"When this exists, that comes to be; from the arising of this,
  that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be;
  from the cessation of this, that ceases."* This is the engine — a
  doctrine of **conditionality**, weaker than strict causation,
  stronger than mere correlation.
- **4.2** The twelve *nidānas* (links). Big-picture map first:
  ignorance → formations → consciousness → name-and-form → six sense
  bases → contact → feeling → craving → clinging → becoming → birth
  → aging-and-death. We then walk it in three groups.
- **4.3** **Links 1–3.** *Avijjā* (ignorance, specifically of the
  four truths) → *saṅkhārā* (volitional formations) → *viññāṇa*
  (consciousness). How not-seeing-clearly drives habit-formation
  drives the next moment of awareness.
- **4.4** **Links 4–7.** *Nāma-rūpa* (name-and-form, mind-and-body) →
  *saḷāyatana* (six sense bases) → *phassa* (contact) → *vedanā*
  (feeling-tone). The objective side of a moment of experience.
- **4.5** **Links 8–12.** *Taṇhā* (craving) → *upādāna* (clinging) →
  *bhava* (becoming) → *jāti* (birth) → *jarā-maraṇa* (aging-and-
  death). Where the engine of suffering closes its loop.
- **4.6** **Two readings of the twelve links.** The *three-life*
  interpretation (classical, includes literal rebirth: links 1–2 past
  life, 3–10 this life, 11–12 next life) vs. the *moment-to-moment*
  interpretation (each link is happening right now in any episode of
  experience). The canon supports both; modern readers usually need
  the second to take the doctrine seriously.
- **4.7** **Cessation is the same chain, run in reverse.** *"When
  ignorance ceases, formations cease; when formations cease,
  consciousness ceases…"* — this is what the Third Noble Truth
  actually means in mechanism.
- **4.8** **Sutta reading: DN 15 — *Mahānidāna Sutta*
  ("The Great Discourse on Causation").** The Buddha to Ānanda:
  *"Deep is this dependent origination, and deep does it appear."*
  Read for the texture, not memorization.

## Phase 5 — Mind, Intention, and Karma
- **5.1** Three words for "mind": *citta*, *mano*, *viññāṇa*. They
  often substitute for each other in the suttas, but each has a
  flavor: *citta* (heart-mind, the affective center), *mano* (mind
  as the sixth sense-base, the cognizing function), *viññāṇa*
  (consciousness of an object via any of six bases). Why early
  Buddhism does not posit a single "mind" entity behind these.
- **5.2** *Kamma* (Skt. *karma*) defined precisely: **intention**
  (*cetanā*). The famous AN 6.63 line: *"Intention, monks, is what
  I call kamma. For having intended, one acts by body, speech, and
  mind."* Karma is not "what happens to you," it is your willed
  action and its conditioning effect on future mental states.
- **5.3** The rebirth question, philosophically. If there is no
  unchanging self, what is "reborn"? The canonical answer: a stream
  of conditioned mental events, no soul required. The candle-flame
  analogy: the flame at midnight isn't the same flame as at sunset,
  but it isn't a different flame either — it is conditioned by it.
  The course presents rebirth as the canon's view, examines its
  internal coherence, and does not require the learner to *believe*
  it.
- **5.4** **The unanswered questions (*avyākata*).** In MN 63 and
  MN 72, the Buddha refuses to answer ten metaphysical questions
  (is the world eternal? infinite? does the *tathāgata* exist after
  death?). The simile of the poisoned arrow (MN 63): demanding
  answers to these before practicing is like a wounded man refusing
  treatment until he learns the archer's caste. Philosophically, this
  is a *deflationary* stance — *pace* metaphysics, focus on what is
  to be done.
- **5.5** **Sutta reading: MN 63 — *Cūḷamālukya Sutta*
  ("The Shorter Discourse to Mālukya")**, the poisoned-arrow text.

## Phase 6 — The Six Senses, Contact, and "the All"
- **6.1** The *saḷāyatana* — six sense bases. Eye, ear, nose, tongue,
  body, *and mind*. The mind (*mano*) as a sense organ taking
  thoughts as its objects — a move that changes everything about how
  experience gets analyzed.
- **6.2** *Phassa* (contact). The meeting of three: sense organ +
  object + the corresponding consciousness. Contact is not the
  organ touching the object — it's the *three-way conjunction* that
  produces a moment of cognition.
- **6.3** **"The All" — *sabba* — SN 35.23.** The Buddha's striking
  reduction: *"Mendicants, I will teach you the all. The eye and
  sights, the ear and sounds, the nose and smells, the tongue and
  tastes, the body and touches, the mind and ideas. This is called
  the all."* That is: all of "existence" for purposes of the path is
  the field of six-sense experience. Compare to Western
  phenomenology (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty) at intuition level — a one-
  paragraph aside, not a digression.
- **6.4** Why this matters: it puts the path's entire object of
  inquiry inside experience, and refuses to speculate about what
  lies "behind" it. This is the deflationary move of Phase 5.4 made
  systematic.

## Phase 7 — Nibbāna and the Arahant
- **7.1** *Nibbāna* (Skt. *nirvāṇa*), literally "extinguishing" — as
  a flame goes out. What is extinguished? Not the person, not
  experience, not consciousness — but the **three fires** of *rāga*
  (greed), *dosa* (hatred), and *moha* (delusion). The image is
  precise: when a fire's fuel is exhausted, it goes out — peacefully,
  not violently.
- **7.2** *Sa-upādisesa* vs. *anupādisesa* — *nibbāna* "with
  remainder" (the arahant who has extinguished the fires but is
  still alive, with the five aggregates continuing) vs. "without
  remainder" (at the death of the arahant, the aggregates do not
  re-arise). Carefully: this is not annihilationism, because there
  was no permanent self to annihilate (anattā again).
- **7.3** Why the Buddha said little *positive* about *nibbāna*. The
  *negative theology* of the canon — *nibbāna* described almost
  exclusively by negation: the unborn, the unconditioned, the
  deathless, the unmade (Ud 8.3). Why this is structurally similar
  to apophatic theology in the Christian mystics (one paragraph,
  honest comparison; we will not push it).
- **7.4** The *arahant* — the philosophical endpoint of the path. A
  person in whom the three fires are extinguished and dependent
  origination's loop is broken. Not omniscient, not divine. The
  *tathāgata* ("thus-gone-one") as the term the Buddha used for
  himself.
- **7.5** A common misreading: *nibbāna* as a *place* one *goes* to,
  or a heaven. Why the canon resists both readings.

## Phase 8 — Abhidhamma: the Systematization
- **8.1** What the *Abhidhamma* is: a ~3rd-c.-BCE scholastic
  reorganization of the suttas. Same doctrines, completely different
  form — taxonomy, lists, definitions, and a fine-grained analysis
  of mental events. Not a separate teaching; a *systematization*.
- **8.2** *Dhammas* (lower-case-d, plural) — the irreducible
  "events" or "factors" of experience. Distinguish from *Dhamma*
  (the teaching) — same word, different uses, contextually
  disambiguated.
- **8.3** **Momentariness (*khaṇa*).** The Theravāda Abhidhamma's
  most distinctive claim: each *dhamma* lasts only a moment, then a
  new one arises. A "thing" is a rapid succession of momentary
  events that resemble each other. Hume and the bundle theory of
  self land in roughly the same place — note the parallel.
- **8.4** Why this matters for the next course. The Mahāyāna course
  will open with **Madhyamaka** (Nāgārjuna), whose critique is
  *aimed at the Abhidhamma*: Nāgārjuna will argue that even the
  *dhammas* the Abhidhamma reifies are themselves empty
  (*śūnya*) of *svabhāva* (own-being). Seeing what is being
  critiqued makes the Mahāyāna critique land.
- **8.5** The four *paramatthas* ("ultimates" of the Abhidhamma) in
  one paragraph: *citta* (consciousness), *cetasika* (mental
  factors), *rūpa* (matter), *nibbāna*. A sketch only — the deep
  dive belongs in a Theravāda-specific course.

## Phase 9 — Putting It Together
- **9.1** The early Buddhist worldview on one page. The student
  writes it themselves; the tutor edits. Test of integration: does
  the student see how the Four Noble Truths, the Three Marks, the
  Five Aggregates, Dependent Origination, and *nibbāna* are *one*
  argument, not five lists?
- **9.2** Capstone (learner's choice — one):
  - **(a)** Take a specific sutta the learner has not yet read
    (suggestions: MN 10 *Satipaṭṭhāna* — read for its philosophical
    content, not as meditation instructions; SN 12.15
    *Kaccānagotta* — "right view" defined; MN 22 *Alagaddūpama* —
    the raft simile; SN 22.86 *Anurādha* — what the Buddha will and
    won't say about the *tathāgata*) and write a 2–3 page
    explication that uses the course's vocabulary precisely.
  - **(b)** Take a Western philosophical position the learner knows
    well (Stoicism, Humean skepticism, Sartrean phenomenology,
    Christian apophatic theology, whatever) and write a careful
    comparison: what does it share with early Buddhism, where does
    it differ, where would the Buddhist diagnose a category error?
  - **(c)** Write a precise account of *anattā* aimed at a smart
    friend who has only encountered Buddhism through pop sources,
    demolishing the standard misreadings.
- **9.3** Where next.
  - The **Mahāyāna philosophy** course (next in this repo) — starts
    with Madhyamaka, which is the direct continuation.
  - **Bhikkhu Bodhi**, *In the Buddha's Words* — best one-volume
    anthology of the Pali Canon, organized doctrinally.
  - **Rupert Gethin**, *The Foundations of Buddhism* — best modern
    academic introduction. Gethin's organization is similar to ours.
  - **Steven Collins**, *Selfless Persons* — the philosophical study
    of *anattā*. Dense; for the learner who wants to push hard on
    Phase 2.3.
  - **SuttaCentral** for translations; **Access to Insight** for the
    older Ṭhānissaro renderings (often more interpretive — useful as
    a second voice).
- **9.4** What we deliberately did *not* cover, and where to find it
  if it later calls: meditation (the *satipaṭṭhāna* / *jhāna*
  literature; a separate practice course); the *Vinaya* (monastic
  rules); the *Jātakas* (past-life tales — folkloric, fun); the
  Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna traditions (their own courses); modern
  secular-Buddhist readings (Stephen Batchelor, etc. — a useful
  conversation partner to the canon, but not the canon).

### Teaching method (applies every lesson)
1. **Recap** the previous concept in 2–3 sentences; ask one quick
   recall question. If shaky, re-teach before continuing.
2. **Introduce one new concept.** Term lands cleanly: name it in
   Pali with diacritics, give the English gloss, say what it is in
   plain language, and say what the doctrine *does* with it (what
   move it enables in the argument).
3. **Pin it to a moment of experience.** Almost every doctrine in
   this course can be made concrete by analyzing a 10-second piece
   of the learner's own ordinary experience (hearing a sound,
   tasting tea, a flash of irritation). Do that. This is the
   course's analogue of "pin every quantum object to a qubit."
4. **Tiny exercise** to verify: usually one of *(i)* state the
   doctrine in your own words without notation; *(ii)* apply it to
   a concrete experience you pick; *(iii)* spot the standard
   misreading. Code is irrelevant here.
5. **Common confusions** when relevant. Single most common in this
   subject: confusing *anattā* with annihilationism (the self that
   was annihilated never existed); confusing *nibbāna* with
   non-existence; reading *kamma* as cosmic justice rather than as
   intention's conditioning effect. Flag explicitly when nearby.
6. **Log** what was covered, the exercise, the learner's answer,
   and a mastery note to `progress.md` and the day's `lessons/`
   file.
7. Keep each session ~1 hour. End with a one-sentence preview of
   next time.

### Mastery criteria

A topic is mastered when the learner can:

1. State the doctrine in their own words, in two or three sentences,
   without Pali (or with Pali, but only because they want to —
   not as a crutch).
2. Apply it to a concrete moment of their own experience and walk
   through the analysis aloud.
3. Spot a deliberately wrong statement of it ("anattā means there is
   no person" — no; it means none of the five aggregates is a
   permanent autonomous self) and correct it.

Record this in the data-dir `progress.md` mastery log.
