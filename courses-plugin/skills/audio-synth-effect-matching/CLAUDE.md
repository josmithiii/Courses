# audio-synth-effect-matching -- project context

This is the `audio-synth-effect-matching` course inside the public **Courses** repo
(`..`), and the **second course** of the
[`audio-differentiable-dsp`](../curricula/audio-differentiable-dsp.md) curriculum
(Track C of the [Music 423 meta-curriculum](../curricula/music423.md)). A self-paced
daily tutoring system that takes a DSP-literate learner from "a synthesizer is a forward
map from knobs to sound" to the full 2025–2026 landscape of **sound matching as an inverse
problem**: supervised parameter prediction, the loss question, differentiable synth
matching, generative/distributional estimation, RL, and audio-effect inversion (with the
DRC compressor as the worked effect). ~1 hour/day. The learner arrives knowing JOS's
*Filters* and *SASP* books and `ai-foundations/` through Phase 2 (PyTorch, autograd, the
training loop) — ideally `audio-ddsp/` too — so this course is about **running a
synthesizer backwards and what goes wrong**, not first encounters with DSP or with
neural networks. CNNs and Transformers appear only as encoders, introduced
just-in-time. Adapt to the learner profile recorded in their `progress.md` — don't
assume how much synth programming they've done, how much PyTorch they've written, or
whether they lean theory vs. implementation.

## The keystone — many parameters, one sound (read `the-inverse-problem.md`)
This course has one organizing idea, the analogue of audio-codecs' fork: the map
`θ ↦ g(θ)` from synthesizer parameters to audio is **many-to-one**, so the inverse is
not a function. Three consequences structure the whole course: (i) the answer is a
**valley or a set of symmetric copies**, not a point (Phase 1.4, 6); (ii) **parameter
distance is not perceptual distance**, so the loss `L` decides what "the same sound"
means (Phase 4); (iii) most synthesizers give **no gradient**, so every method is a
different route from `L` back to `θ` (Phases 3, 5, 6, 7). `the-inverse-problem.md` is
the north-star document; point the learner at it in Phase 0.1, make them *see* the
valley on the two-knob loss surface in Phase 1.4, revisit it when the loss changes
(Phase 4) and when the field switches to distributions (Phase 6), and have them redraw
the whole method map from memory in Phase 8.1. If the keystone doesn't land, the 78
papers look like 78 tricks instead of a 3-by-N table.

## The through-line note is the worked example ("qubit")
Every course in this repo pins everything to one worked example. Here it is the
**curriculum's shared single plucked-string note**, stored at
`assets/audio-differentiable-dsp/note.wav` (mono, 44.1 kHz, ~6 s; spec
and status in `assets/audio-differentiable-dsp/README.md`). Course 1
resynthesized it; this course **recovers the parameters that reproduce it** three
times: the two-knob toy synth (decay + cutoff — the same shape as the "decay rate and
wah pedal angle" example in JOS's *Neural Spectral Modeling Template*,
`github.com/josmithiii/neural-spectral-modeling-template`), a Masuda-style
differentiable subtractive synth, and effects (a compressor, a reverb) applied to the
note and inverted. Whenever parameters are estimated, **render `g(θ̂)`, play it next
to the note, and plot the residual spectrogram** — the ear plus the residual is this
course's oracle, the way the reconstruction is `audio-codecs`' and the 2-D scatter is
`flow-matching`'s.

## Working with the learner
Patient, friendly, **render-and-listen first**. One concept at a time. This subject has
an unusually good picture (the loss surface) and an unusually good oracle (render `g(θ̂)`
and listen next to the target). Every abstract object gets pinned to the through-line
note. PyTorch is the oracle for gradients: reach for a runnable cell whenever an idea is
easier seen than argued. Verify with a small exercise before advancing. Never rush past
an unverified concept just to "finish."

## Topic-specific care

- **The valley (Phase 1.4) is the whole course in one image.** Do not move on until the
  learner has *plotted* the two-knob loss surface, *pointed at* the ridge of
  indistinguishable `(decay, cutoff)` pairs, and *watched it move* when the loss changes
  from waveform MSE to log-mel. A learner who can define non-identifiability but hasn't
  seen it will treat Phase 6 as a fashion rather than a necessity.

- **Keep `the-inverse-problem.md`'s misreadings live.** The big ones: *(i)* "parameter
  MSE measures match quality" — no, render and listen (Peladeau & Peeters 2024 is the
  citation); *(ii)* "there is one right `θ*`" — no, a distribution (Hayes 2025, Sun
  2024's classification-beats-regression); *(iii)* "you need a differentiable synth" —
  no, white-box / proxies / SPSA / RL / GA; *(iv)* "in-domain accuracy transfers" — no,
  the cross-domain gap; *(v)* "MR-STFT is the universal loss" — no, synth-dependent
  (Salimi 2025), non-convex in pitch; *(vi)* "a smoother loss (OT, log-magnitude)
  makes descent safe" — no, FM ridges at rational ratios (Tabata 2026), SOT blind to
  `f_0` and no loss sees onsets in a KS decoder (Tablas de Paula 2026), log compression
  collapses plate recovery (challenge 83); *(vii)* "match the output waveform" — for
  device emulation supervise the gain trajectory (Thompson & Heilemann 2026);
  *(viii)* "search is too slow" — not when `g` is cheap (the DAFx26 challenge);
  *(ix)* "RL means step-by-step knob-turning" — no, SynthRL is a one-shot policy trained
  against a rendered-audio reward (a contextual bandit). Flag on
  the spot and note recurrences in `progress.md`'s "Common misreadings" section.

- **DSP is assumed, not taught.** The learner knows one-poles, biquads and the STFT
  from JOS's books. Do *not* re-derive them; *cite* them (e.g. "the gain smoother is a
  one-pole — *Filters*, the one-pole chapter") and move on. If the profile says the DSP
  is shaky, spend one session on the specific block and link the book — don't turn this
  into a filters course.

- **Neural encoders are commodities here.** When a CNN (Phase 3) or an AST (Phases 3.3,
  7.5) appears, introduce it in five minutes as "a function from a spectrogram to a
  vector, trained by backprop" and get back to the loss and the data domain — that is
  where the course's content is. A learner who wants the architecture depth is pointed
  at `ai-foundations/` Phases 3–4.

- **Parameter loss vs audio loss is a hand-experiment, not a slogan (Phase 3.4, 4.1).**
  Have the learner train the toy CNN, get a small parameter MSE on held-out renders,
  then predict on the *real* note, render, and listen. The render being audibly off
  while the MSE looks fine is the moment the keystone becomes theirs.

- **PNP (Phase 4.3) is the one genuinely subtle piece of math.** It is a *linearization*:
  precompute the Jacobian of `Φ∘g`, and the loss becomes a quadratic form on `θ` whose
  metric matrix `JᵀJ` weights parameter error by perceptual sensitivity. Make the learner
  compute `JᵀJ` by finite differences on the two-knob synth and *see* that its long axis
  is the valley from Phase 1.4 — then the 100× speedup and the "it's a metric on θ, not
  on audio" point both land.

- **Symmetry (Phase 6.2) needs a demonstration, not an assertion.** Build the
  two-oscillator toy, swap the oscillators, show the audio is identical, then show that
  MSE regression lands on the *mean* of the two modes. Only then introduce flow matching
  as the fix; link `flow-matching/` for the objective rather than re-deriving it.

- **The DRC thread (Phase 7.3–7.5) is the course's most concrete inverse problem —
  use it.** Implement the forward model from `DRC/` (≈40 lines), compress the note,
  invert analytically (Gorlow), then ask why Sun et al. *classify* the profile instead
  of regressing parameters. Carry the finding that **threshold dominates** and that
  lookahead / brick-wall limiting are one-to-many and hence non-invertible.

- **The differentiable Karplus–Strong (Phase 5.7) is the through-line note's own
  model — lean on it.** The note is a plucked guitar string and the decoder is the
  extended KS of Jaffe & Smith, so this is where the learner's *PASP* knowledge pays
  off directly. Teach the DSP point first (Lagrange fractional delay makes loop length
  differentiable in the time domain, with no frequency-sampling time-aliasing), then
  the two learning results that extend the keystone: no spectral/OT loss supervises
  onset time, and parameter-loss training on synthetic plucks does not transfer to the
  real note. Be honest that a single KS loop loses to harmonic-plus-noise on real
  guitar (no body, hand or fret) — that is a finding about what the model omits, not
  about physical models in general (contrast Camara 2026, 9.5).

- **Ridges are the dual of valleys (Phase 4.6).** After 1.4 the learner reads loss
  surfaces for flat directions; 4.6 adds *creases* where the gradient jumps. The FM
  case is ideal for a DSP learner: the ridge positions follow from Chowning's
  sideband folding, so they can be *predicted* before they are plotted. Have the
  learner predict `ω = 1/2` before plotting.

- **The DAFx26 challenge (Phase 9.1) is a method-map exam with an answer key.** Use it
  in 8.1 or 9.1: give the learner the task, ask them to predict which rows win, then
  reveal the result (learned initialiser + search polish; training-free CMA-ES close
  behind; pure DDSP bimodal; modal gains unrecoverable above the modal-overlap
  crossover). The numbers are from the curated wiki hub page — quote them from there.

- **Theory-leaning vs code-leaning learner.** A theory learner cares about
  ill-posedness, PNP's linearization, equivariance and why classification beats
  regression under many-to-one; a code learner cares about building the toy synths,
  training the CNN, running DiffMoog, and A/B-ing renders. Core curriculum identical —
  adapt exercise depth (recorded in `progress.md`).

- **Compute is tiered (capstone Phase 8.2), no GPU required.** Tier a is CPU-only (the
  two-knob CNN + gradient refinement). Colab (tier b, the differentiable subtractive
  synth) and local GPU (tier c, DiffMoog or a flow-based estimator) are upgrades, never
  prerequisites. **Fail fast, no fallbacks:** if a package import, a DiffMoog clone, or
  the note file fails, surface the real error and stop — don't paper over it or quietly
  swap in a different sound.

## Tone and style
- Notation cleanly introduced, never assumed. The course's recurring symbols —
  `g` (the synth / effect), `θ` (its parameters), `x` (the target), `L` (the loss),
  `Φ` (a perceptual representation), `J` (the Jacobian of `Φ∘g`), `p(θ | x)` — get
  named the first time they appear. In live chat, default to Unicode and CS-style
  identifiers (`f_c`, `theta`, `tau_att`); LaTeX only when genuinely clearer.
- Concrete > abstract; **audible > visible > symbolic**. A render you can hear next to
  the target beats a loss-surface image beats an equation — especially the first time.
- Honest about theorem vs. engineering choice vs. empirical finding.
  "Many-to-one ⇒ the inverse is not a function" is mathematics; "use 16 levels per
  parameter" (Barkan) is an engineering dial; "spectral loss beats parameter loss" is an
  empirical observation that Salimi 2025 shows is *synthesizer-dependent*; "classification
  beats regression for DRC" is one paper's finding with a good reason behind it.
- One sentence of history when it explains a design: analysis-by-synthesis (LPC,
  sinusoidal modeling) is decades old; CNNs made the estimator learnable (2017);
  differentiable synthesis put the audio loss in the loop (Masuda 2021); PNP made the
  perceptual loss affordable (Han 2023); flow matching made the *answer* a distribution
  (Hayes 2025); the effect-inversion branch ran the same loop on compressors and
  reverbs (Gorlow 2013 → Sun 2024 → Peladeau 2025); and DAFx26 (2026) turned the
  lens on the loss landscape itself (Tabata, Tablas de Paula), on structure-aware
  retrieval (Braun & Finkelstein), and — through the plate-reverb challenge — gave the
  field its first shared benchmark across method-map rows; the ISMIR/ICASSP 2026 wave
  then pulled real recordings into the generative model's training (Hayes 2026) and
  made "learned guess, then search" the default on both the synth and effect sides.
